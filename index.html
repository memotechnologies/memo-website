The problem

Every other tool waits for the work to arrive in a pattern.

A booked call. A recorded meeting. A clean CRM record. Most of a rep’s week arrives in none of those shapes — it’s a phone call from the car, five minutes in a corridor, a reply on LinkedIn. The pattern-shaped tools miss it, and the rep pays for it twice: once in admin, and once in the deal.

i.

The touchpoint leaves no trace.

Closing takes one to five conversations, most of them on the phone — and most teams run no note-taker at all. There is no transcript to summarise, so there is nothing for the rest of the category to work from.

ii.

So the CRM stays empty.

Notes don’t fill fields. Reps update the CRM by hand, late, or never — and the pipeline everyone forecasts from is only ever as good as what somebody remembered to type.

iii.

And the follow-up falls on the rep.

The recap, the invite, the task, the nudge two weeks later. All of it lands on the person who just spent an hour actually selling — usually at nine in the evening.

Why Memo

Everyone else assumes something your team doesn’t do.

Not a feature grid — a list of assumptions. Each of these categories works beautifully, right up to the point where the work stops arriving in the shape it expects.

What the alternatives take for granted

  • CRM-native AI Assumes the work happens inside their walls. It sprawls to Workspace, Slack and the phone.
  • Voice capture tools Assumes the rep stops and dictates a memo. But capture isn’t assembly: one memo, one field.
  • Agentic sales layers Assumes the CRM already holds good data. The data never got captured in the first place.
  • AI-native CRMs Assumes you’ll replace your CRM. Nobody rips out HubSpot or Pipedrive to try a startup.
  • The one you built internally Assumes whoever built it keeps maintaining it. Gartner expects 40%+ of agent projects to be scrapped by 2027.

Where that leaves Memo

  • It takes the touchpoint nobody else can use A phone call, a note dictated walking to the car, a line typed between meetings. No recording, no clean input, no form.
  • One piece of context, many actions The whole trick: one note becomes the CRM update, the email, the invite and the tasks that follow. That is what pilots quote back to us.
  • It glues the scattered pieces into one account picture CRM, calendar, inbox and prior meetings read together, so the draft knows what the deal already knew.
  • The rep stays in the driver’s seat Priced and framed like hiring a worker, not like another seat of software — and it never acts behind your back.
How it works

Monitors. Thinks. Does the work. Learns.

One loop, four parts — and it runs whether or not anyone talks to it. Memo is a single agent with about thirty tools and one gate: it can read anything you’ve connected, and it can only ever propose the write.

Monitors.

Webhooks from the CRM, your calendar and your inbox, plus a pass over your pipeline every half hour. It sees the deal stalling, the call nobody logged, the meeting that got declined.

Thinks.

Works out what context is missing, whether this is worth interrupting you for, and which surface to put it on. Every card it raises carries the reason it exists.

Does the work.

Contacts, companies, deals, meeting logs, tasks, calendar invites, email drafts — deduplicated, stage fields validated, written across every connected system in one pass.

Learns.

Every approve, edit, reject and dismissal is recorded with its reason and read back into the next draft. Or just tell it: “in situations like this, do that.”

Control

One gate. Everything goes through it.

An assistant that writes to your CRM and drafts from your mailbox is only worth having if you can say exactly what it is allowed to do. Three of those answers are structural — properties of how Memo is built, not policies somebody has to remember to apply.

i.

Nothing changes or sends without you.

There are no direct write tools in Memo — none. Every state change, from every surface, funnels through a single proposal step. Approval isn’t a setting; it’s the only path there is.

ii.

Undo on anything it created.

Every row that added a record carries an Undo that removes exactly that record. Rows that changed an existing value or sent an invite carry none — we won’t offer an Undo that quietly does nothing.

iii.

Your data can’t reach another customer.

Isolation is enforced at the database role, not in application code: every query runs inside a row-level-security context the app cannot bypass. What never reaches the model can’t be talked out of it.

And what we deliberately don’t build: we don’t record your calls, we don’t become your phone system, and we don’t ask you to replace the CRM you already pay for. If audio is ever involved, it’s audio someone else recorded and chose to share.

The proactive side

Standing work you delegate once, and stop thinking about.

Routines are how you hand Memo a job for good. You write one the way you’d ask a colleague — “every weekday morning, draft a check-in for each of my trial deals”, “when a deal moves to Proposal, prep the pricing brief” — and it runs on a clock or on an event from then on. It still drafts, and you still approve. The work lands in your Workspace and on your day, whether or not you went looking.

The web app is where the work is guaranteed to land. Slack is a delivery channel on top of it — a morning digest, a heads-up DM, a proposal you can approve without leaving the thread. Turn it on if that’s where your team already lives, and leave it off if it isn’t.
